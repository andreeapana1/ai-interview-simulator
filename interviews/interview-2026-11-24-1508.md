**Question 1:** Our data team keeps talking about adding a *vector database* to our AI setup. In simple terms, what is a vector database, and what kind of data does it store?

**Candidate:** A vector database stores data by its meaning. An AI model turns text, like documents or emails, into lists of numbers called embeddings, and texts with similar meanings get similar numbers. The database stores those numbers, usually together with the original text.

It's mainly used to find similar content. For example, it can find our 'business trip reimbursement' policy when someone asks about 'travel expenses.' That lets an AI assistant look up the right company documents before it answers.

**Interviewer:** Thanks.

**Question 2:** You mentioned embeddings. Why do AI models need to turn words into embeddings in the first place? And what does it mean when two embeddings are "close together"?

**Candidate:** Computers can't understand words directly. They only work with numbers. An embedding turns a word or sentence into a list of numbers that captures its meaning, so the model can compare and calculate with it.

When two embeddings are 'close together,' their meanings are similar. 'Invoice' and 'bill' would be close, while 'invoice' and 'holiday' would be far apart. So closeness is how the model measures similarity in meaning, even when the words are different.

**Interviewer:** Okay, next one.

**Question 3:** The "T" in GPT stands for *Transformer*. Before transformers, models like recurrent neural networks (RNNs) were used for language. How does a transformer's attention mechanism work differently from an RNN, and why did that give transformers such an advantage?

**Candidate:** An RNN reads text one word at a time, in order, like a person reading word by word and trying to remember everything so far. In a long text, it tends to forget the early parts. And because it goes step by step, it's slow to train.

A transformer uses attention instead. It looks at all the words at once, and for each word it works out which other words matter most for its meaning. In 'The invoice was late because it was missing a PO number,' attention helps the model link 'it' to 'invoice.'

That gave transformers two big advantages. First, they handle long texts and connections between distant words much better. Second, they process words in parallel, so they can be trained much faster on huge amounts of data. That's what made today's large language models possible.

**Interviewer:** Thanks.

**Question 4:** The Anthropic Academy course describes four properties of generative AI. Pick **one** of them and explain how the *same* mechanism gives the model both a capability and a limitation.

**Candidate:** I'll pick next token prediction.

The model writes its answer one word at a time, each time guessing what's most likely to come next based on patterns it learned in training.

That's what makes it so capable. It can write fluent, natural text on almost any topic, like summarising a report or drafting an email, because it has seen so many patterns of how language works.

The same mechanism is also the limitation. It's predicting what sounds right, not looking up what is right. It works more like a very advanced autocomplete than a search engine. So when it doesn't know something, it can still produce an answer that sounds confident but is wrong, which is what we call a hallucination.

In practice, that means I'd use AI for drafting and analysis, but always check facts and numbers against the source.

**Interviewer:** Okay, next one.

**Question 5:** Imagine our legal and finance team wants to paste a 300-page supplier contract into an AI chat and ask it to find every payment penalty clause. Thinking about the model's context window, what could go wrong, and what would you do to get a reliable result?

**Candidate:** A 300-page contract might not fit in the context window, so parts could be cut off. Even if it fits, the model can miss details in the middle of long texts, or invent a clause that isn't there.

To get a reliable result, I'd split the contract into smaller sections and ask the same clear question for each one. I'd ask for clause numbers and exact quotes, so we can check every answer against the original. I'd also run a simple keyword search for words like 'penalty' and 'late payment' as a double-check. Finally, someone from legal should review the list before we rely on it.

**Interviewer:** Thanks. Last question.

**Question 6:** Our finance department is choosing between a regular LLM and a reasoning model (LRM). One use is writing short product descriptions; another is tracing unusual cash flows across several subsidiaries to spot inconsistencies. Which model would you use for each, and why? Think about the trade-offs.

**Candidate:** For the product descriptions, I'd use a regular LLM. It's a simple, creative writing task that doesn't need deep thinking. A regular model is faster and cheaper, and the quality is just as good for this kind of work.

For tracing unusual cash flows, I'd use a reasoning model. That task has many steps: following money between subsidiaries, comparing amounts and checking that everything adds up. A reasoning model works through the problem step by step before answering, so it makes fewer logic mistakes on complex analysis.

The trade-off is that reasoning models are slower and more expensive, so it doesn't make sense to use them for simple tasks. I'd match the model to the task. Even with a reasoning model, the cash flow findings should be checked by a person, because it can still make mistakes, and in finance those mistakes matter.

**Interviewer:** Thanks. We have a little time left, so here's one bonus question.

**Question 7:** Our accounts payable team wants to automate invoice approval. Some invoices are routine and match the purchase order exactly; others are messy, with unclear descriptions or missing details. How would you split the work between fixed business rules and an AI agent, and why?

**Candidate:** I'd use both, each for what it does best.

Routine invoices that match the purchase order exactly should go through fixed business rules. The rules check that the vendor exists, the amounts match the PO and the goods receipt, and it isn't a duplicate. If everything passes and the amount is under a set limit, it's approved automatically. Rules are fast, predictable and easy to audit, which matters a lot when we're paying money out.

For the messy invoices, I'd bring in an AI agent. It can read unclear descriptions, pull out details from scanned documents, suggest the right PO or cost centre, and flag anything unusual, like a new vendor or an amount much higher than normal. But the AI shouldn't approve payment on its own. It prepares the invoice and explains what's wrong or missing, and then a person makes the decision.

So rules handle the clean cases and control the approval, and AI helps people with the messy ones. I'd start by letting the AI only make suggestions, measure how accurate it is, and then automate more step by step.
